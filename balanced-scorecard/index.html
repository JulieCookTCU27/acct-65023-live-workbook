    4Test It and Get Started

    The Weakest Link: Arrow 9, Families’ Trust → Filled Hours

    The map assumes trust turns into attendance. But Medicaid families often miss visits for reasons trust cannot fix: transport, shift work, illness. Trust can rise while visits stay flat.

    Evidence over six months that it isn’t working: recommend scores and fourth-visit rates rise, but family cancellations and no-shows stay near 20.8% of bookings and therapist hours delivered stay near 67%. Check the reasons coded on each family cancellation: if most are transport or work, the fix is logistics (telehealth, evening slots, ride help), not trust.

    The measure most likely to improve while the strategy fails: scheduled visits completed. It rises if schedulers book fewer visits for families who often cancel, or quietly rebook cancellations, while children get less therapy. Watch it beside total completed visits and the share of children who reach a fourth visit.

    Data Checks

    • Break-even ties out. Average visits Jan–Apr are (2,338 + 2,931 + 3,220 + 3,004) ÷ 4 = 2,873.25, matching the Session 1 data. A loss of $4,594 on 146.75 visits short of break-even implies about $31.30 of contribution per visit, and $95,000 of fixed clinic costs ÷ 3,020 visits = $31.46. The two figures agree within rounding.
    • Scheduled visits completed (73.1%) is worked out as 100% − 20.8% − 6.1%. It assumes cancellations and no-shows are the only reasons a booking is not completed. Confirm it against the EMR.
    • Turnover is a four-month figure. 2 of 25 in four months is 8%, about 24% a year if the pace holds. Compare annual rates, not four-month ones, with industry figures.
    • “Providers” read as therapists in L1. If the 25 includes non-therapist providers, the measure needs rewording.
    • Course demo: the professor’s “all measures” and “numbers” pages show a second, empty row under Keep the Appointments We Schedule. Here it is filled with a leading measure, so every perspective now has a leading measure.
    • Course content conflict: slide 11 says non-financial measures lead; the Balanced Scorecard explainer says non-financial does not automatically mean leading. This page follows the explainer: leading or lagging is relative to each measure’s own objective.

    Improved Prompts

    The course prompts ask the AI to write its own prompt, then check it against a list. These versions put the checklist, the formulas and the tests into the prompt itself, so the first build already meets them. Fill in the brackets, paste, and the AI stops after each stage for you to check.

    What changed from the course prompts

      

    • Rules up front: the scorecard checklist (two objectives per row, bottom-up arrows, at least one leading measure, owners who can influence the measure) is written into the prompt instead of being checked afterward.
      • 

    • Exact definitions: each measure must state its numerator, denominator and period, so the AI can’t invent a vague measure.
      • 

    • Built-in tests: every measure gets a gaming test (how it could improve while its objective gets worse), and every number is checked against the figures it should tie to.
      • 

    • Fixed formulas: the variance prompt states the course formulas and sign convention, so the AI can’t use a different market-share or price-variance convention. It also requires the reconciliation to tie out to $0.
      • 

    • Stop after each stage: the AI waits for you to check before going on. This keeps the course’s Ask, Check, Build rhythm in one prompt.
